RESEARCH DIRECTIONS

Thermal noise:
from material to detector.

My research brings together silicon characterisation, mechanical loss measurements and optical thermometry to understand the material and temperature dependence of detector thermal noise.

01 / MATERIALS

Silicon characterisation

Characterising crystal quality, electrical properties and optical behaviour to inform studies of absorption, heating and dissipation. These measurements support the material model needed to assess thermal noise.

ResistivityCrystal qualityBirefringence
02 / MECHANICS

Mechanical loss

Developing electrostatic excitation and ringdown experiments to measure mechanical quality factors. Through the fluctuation-dissipation theorem, mechanical loss connects directly to Brownian thermal noise; detector predictions also require the relevant geometry and frequency dependence.

Q factorElectrostatic driveThermal noise
03 / OPTICS

Optical thermometry

Using the silicon test mass itself as an optical cavity to explore bulk thermometry. Tracking thermal expansion and refractive index changes helps establish the temperature conditions needed to interpret loss measurements and thermal noise.

InterferometryRefractive indexThermal expansion

WHY THERMAL NOISE MATTERS

Small fluctuations.
A fundamental sensitivity limit.

Thermally driven motion in mirrors and suspensions can mask a gravitational wave signal. Mechanical dissipation sets the associated Brownian noise through the fluctuation-dissipation theorem. Temperature fluctuations can also change dimensions and refractive index. My work within ET CRISTAL studies the material properties and experimental conditions needed to understand these contributions in cryogenic silicon.

CODE & EXPERIMENTS

Experiments and models
with thermal noise in view.

From optical geometry and material mapping to thermal modelling, each project addresses part of the path from a silicon sample to a reliable detector noise model.

OPTICAL MODELLING

Silicon slab ray tracing

An interactive ray tracing tool for separating front and back surface reflections in silicon. It supports the optical geometry of bulk thermometry, which helps establish the operating temperature used in loss and thermal noise studies.

Explore the repository
MATERIAL CHARACTERISATION

Resistivity mapping

A proposed four-point probe measurement plan for comparing electrical uniformity across silicon samples. Resistivity provides context for carrier-related absorption and heating studies; it does not by itself determine mechanical loss or thermal noise.

Explore the repository
ATOMISTIC MODELLING · RESEARCH DIRECTION

Silicon modelling with LAMMPS

A proposed molecular dynamics study of silicon, exploring how atomic structure and temperature affect elastic response and heat transport. The aim is to connect microscopic behaviour with material inputs for thermal noise models.

Classical molecular dynamics requires careful validation at cryogenic temperatures, where quantum effects become important. A thermal conductivity calculation alone is not a prediction of detector Brownian noise.

LAMMPS thermal transport methods
EARLIER WORK · CARDIFF UNIVERSITY

Laser heating and thermal compensation

My MSc project investigated shorter wavelength laser heating for thermal compensation using COMSOL. It considered absorption and heat deposition in interferometer optics.

This work provides a background in temperature distributions and optical distortion. Thermal compensation addresses heating-induced distortion, complementing studies of the temperature fluctuations that produce thermal noise.

EXPERIMENT DEVELOPMENT

Electrostatic excitation and ringdown

Developing a non-contact drive for mechanical loss measurements in silicon. Separating intrinsic material loss from suspension, gas and actuator-related damping is essential before using measured quality factors to inform thermal noise models.

CRYOGENIC SUSPENSIONS

Silicon suspension elements

Research interests in monocrystalline silicon suspension elements bring together strength, surface quality, heat extraction and mechanical dissipation. The challenge is to support and cool a test mass while limiting suspension thermal noise.

ABOUT ME

From sustainable engineering
to gravitational wave astronomy.

I am a researcher with a background in mechanical engineering, thermal and green engineering, physics and astrophysics. At the University of Liège’s Precision Mechatronics Laboratory, I contribute to Einstein Telescope research through ET CRISTAL, studying cryogenic silicon, mechanical loss and optical thermometry with thermal noise as a central focus.

Earlier in my career, I worked as a scientist at India’s Ministry of New and Renewable Energy (MNRE). During this period, I was also based in Oxford and undertook part-time study at the University of Oxford. My work in sustainable solar technologies includes patents relating to transparent and flexible solar panels.

I subsequently resigned from my government position to pursue astrophysics, completing an MSc in Astrophysics at Cardiff University before beginning my doctoral research in Liège. This transition brought my experience in thermal engineering and materials into the study of gravitational wave detector technology.

Alongside my research, I have founded and continue to manage firms focused on green engineering and advanced sustainable technologies, with operations in the United States, United Kingdom and India.

Science communication is an important part of my work. I value collaborations that connect people with science and make research accessible beyond the laboratory.
